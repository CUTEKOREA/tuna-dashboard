The Pacific Value Capture Playbook

The world's largest tuna resource is biologically healthy and economically captured elsewhere. What the 2025 tariff realignment changes, and what it does not.

The thesis in three sentences

Most tuna analysis opens with a stock crisis. There isn't one — 97% of the global catch now comes from stocks at healthy abundance, and the four Western and Central Pacific target stocks show a zero percent probability of overfishing. The binding constraint in this industry is not biological.

It is structural. The Pacific supplies more than half the world's tuna and retains roughly one sixth of first-sale value and effectively none of what follows. Six coastal states now depend on that single rent stream for between 28% and 98% of government revenue.

The August 2025 US tariff realignment is the first event in two decades capable of moving where processing sits. Whether coastal states capture any of that movement is the investable question.

01

Executive summary

One — the resource is not the problem, and saying otherwise has misdirected a decade of capital. The 2024 Western and Central Pacific catch of 3,059,005 tonnes was the highest on record. Skipjack set a record and the 2025 assessment places the stock near target. Bigeye catch fell to roughly 119,000 t, among the lowest since the 1990s, with purse-seine bigeye at about 34,000 t — the lowest since 1990, reflecting a shift toward free-school sets. All four WCPO target stocks are assessed healthy.

Two — the value gap is the problem. The WCPO purse seine fishery has been valued at roughly US$2.8bn, against PNA access fees in the order of US$450–470m. Coastal states capture something near a sixth of first-sale value and essentially nothing from processing, branding, distribution or retail — which is where the margin lives. The FFA's 2026 trade report states the structure plainly: more than half of global supply originates in the WCPO while processing and distribution sit outside it.

Three — the tariff realignment is redrawing the processing map. US reciprocal tariffs applied from August 2025 hit the two dominant canning origins, while Ecuador's lower rate and proximity improved its relative position and American Samoa production remained inside the tariff wall. Bangkok skipjack CFR moved between roughly US$1,580 and US$1,720 per tonne through 2025 on tariff uncertainty rather than on supply.

The investable implication is narrow. The opportunity is not to add purse seine capacity — the resource is fully allocated through an effort-control regime and additional vessel days are a cost, not an asset. The opportunity is in the layer coastal states have never held: onshore processing with secured raw supply, traceability that survives buyer audit, and market access that does not depend on a single tariff jurisdiction.

02

Reality check: four corrections

Tuna is unusually badly served by secondary sources. The corrections below are not marginal — each one changes an investment case.

Common assumption

Tuna stocks are in crisis and supply is structurally constrained.

Position as at 2026

97% of the global catch comes from stocks at healthy abundance, and close to 100% from stocks not experiencing overfishing. All four WCPO target stocks are healthy with a zero percent overfishing probability A.

Common assumption

Bigeye pressure from purse seine FAD fishing continues to worsen.

Position as at 2026

Purse-seine bigeye catch fell to roughly 34,000 t — the lowest since 1990 — as fleets shifted toward free-school sets. Total bigeye is near its lowest level since the 1990s A.

Widely republished

Bangkok skipjack spiked to roughly US$1,845/t in August 2025 on the US tariff.

What trade reporting shows

We could not corroborate that figure. Trade press placed Bangkok CFR at roughly US$1,600–1,700/t in October 2025, having fallen below $1,600 in May on tariff uncertainty B. The spike claim traces to a secondary blog and is not used here.

Common assumption

Japanese sashimi demand is a stable long-term outlet for longline catch.

Peer-reviewed finding

Per-capita sashimi tuna consumption peaked around 1,100 g in 2002 and has declined since. Cohort effects and ageing drive a structural fall to roughly 112,000 t total demand by 2050, with inbound tourism offsetting only part of it B.

The first correction matters most, because it inverts the standard investment framing. If the resource were the constraint, the scarce asset would be quota and the winning position would be upstream. It is not. The stocks are healthy, effort is capped by agreement rather than by biology, and the scarce asset is therefore processing capacity with secured supply and clean market access — a downstream position, not an upstream one.

03

The value capture gap

This is the structural fact the entire investment case rests on, and it is worth stating without decoration.

The Western and Central Pacific supplies more than half the world's tuna. The purse seine fishery within it has been valued at approximately US$2.8bn, itself about 54% of total WCPO catch value near US$5.3bn A. Against that, PNA access fees have run in the order of US$450–470m A.

Coastal states therefore retain roughly a sixth of first-sale value, and essentially none of the processing, branding, distribution and retail margin that follows. The FFA's 2026 trade report identifies exactly this: supply originates in the Pacific while processing and distribution sit outside it, limiting coastal-state value capture A.

Why this is a fiscal question, not only a commercial one

Access fees are not incremental revenue for these states. They are 28% to 98% of government revenue across six PNA countries B. A single commodity rent, negotiated annually, funds the state.

That dependence cuts two ways for an investor, and both belong in the model. It makes coastal governments highly motivated to move up the chain — which is the opening. It also makes them highly sensitive to anything that threatens near-term fee revenue, which constrains how fast concessions can be granted to onshore processors. A proposal that reduces vessel-day revenue in year one to build a factory that pays in year five is asking a finance ministry to fund the gap.

A disclosed inconsistency

Sources do not agree on the recent trajectory of access fees. VDS licence revenue is documented rising from US$64m in 2010 to US$470m by 2016 A. More recent commentary describes fees at "over US$300m currently" C — which would imply a material decline rather than continued growth.

We could not resolve this from public sources. The difference may be definitional (VDS licence fees versus total access revenue versus PNA-only versus all FFA members). Any model that depends on the fee trend should establish this directly with FFA or PNA rather than rely on either published figure. We show both and mark the interval as uncertain rather than drawing a confident line through it.

One figure that is not the access fee pool and is sometimes confused with it: PNA Office operating revenue, reported at US$7.42m for 2024. That is the secretariat's own budget, not the rent.

04

The tariff realignment

From August 2025 the United States applied reciprocal tariffs that fell unevenly across the canned tuna supply base. The effect is not a demand shock; it is a relative cost reordering among processing origins, and it is the first force in two decades with the power to move where cans are made.

Processing origins under the 2025 tariff structure
OriginPosition after August 2025Consequence
ThailandDominant canning hub, now tariffed into its largest export marketCost disadvantage into the US; redirection pressure toward EU and Middle East B

EcuadorLower tariff rate plus proximity to the US market and to Eastern Pacific raw supplyRelative winner. Manta ex-vessel around US$1,650/t with a persistent US$300–350/t discount to Bangkok B
American SamoaUS territory — production sits inside the tariff wallStructural advantage for canners with capacity there B

Two planning conclusions follow. First, the redirected volume lands somewhere. Thai and Vietnamese product pushed out of the US must find EU and Middle Eastern buyers, which intensifies price competition in exactly the markets a new coastal-state processor would target. A business case built on EU access should assume a more crowded market than 2024 implied.

Second, a tariff-driven advantage is a policy asset, not a structural one. Ecuador's position and American Samoa's position both rest on a schedule that can change. Underwriting a twenty-year asset on a tariff differential is the same error as underwriting on a 46% figure that was never implemented.

05

Demand: where the catch actually goes

Tuna demand splits into two businesses that behave nothing alike, and conflating them is the most common analytical error in this sector.

Canned: volume, resilient, price-led

Global tuna trade volume rose approximately 28% in 2024 while trade value rose only 3.32% A — a volume-led year with unit values falling, driven by canned demand. US imports of canned and prepared tuna reached 230,557 tonnes, an all-time high A. In the first quarter of 2025 fresh and frozen tuna trade fell 7.65% year on year A, as higher-value fresh demand softened while canned held.

The read is that canned tuna behaves as an affordable-protein staple and gains share when consumers trade down. That is a defensive characteristic and it is the segment most exposed to the tariff reordering above.

Sashimi: structurally declining, and the decline is demographic

Japanese per-capita sashimi tuna consumption peaked near 1,100 g in 2002 and has fallen since. The peer-reviewed projection is a structural decline to roughly 112,000 t of total demand by 2050, driven by cohort effects — younger generations eat less seafood — compounded by ageing. Inbound tourism offsets part of the domestic fall but cannot close it B.

This is a hard finding for longline economics. A fleet whose value proposition is sashimi-grade fish for Japan is serving a market with a demographic ceiling that no marketing effort reverses. The strategic responses are to develop sashimi-grade demand in the US, EU and emerging Asian markets, or to reposition the fleet. Neither is quick, and both need to start before the decline is visible in a single year's landings.

06

Unit economics: what actually decides returns

Coastal-state processing fails for predictable reasons, and none of them are the fish.

Operating variables ranked by influence
VariableWhy it decides the outcome
Secured raw supplyThe dominant variable. A cannery without contracted landings competes for fish against Bangkok on the open market and loses. Supply security, not plant efficiency, determines utilisation
Plant utilisationCanning is a high fixed-cost operation. Below roughly break-even utilisation the plant destroys value regardless of margin per case
Vessel day costUnder an effort-control regime, days are the licence to operate and their price is negotiated, not competed. Day cost enters the raw material cost directly
Cold chain integrityDetermines the grade the catch can be sold into. A break moves fish from sashimi to cannery grade and destroys the price difference
Market access statusEU preference eligibility, US tariff position and buyer audit standing are worth more per tonne than most operating improvements
Labour and compliance standingForced-labour and IUU findings remove market access outright. This is a binary risk, not a graduated one

Where capital gets destroyed

  • Building the plant before securing the fish. The recurring failure in coastal-state processing. Raw supply agreements must precede construction, not follow it.
  • Underwriting on a tariff differential. Tariff schedules change on political timelines, not investment timelines.
  • Treating compliance as a cost line. Traceability and labour standards are access conditions. Losing them does not reduce margin; it removes the customer.
  • Assuming Japan absorbs sashimi-grade output. See section 05. That assumption has a demographic expiry date.
07

Phased entry

Raw supply security, buyer willingness to pay a coastal-origin premium, and compliance readiness are all systematically overestimated without field evidence. The sequence below places verification before capital.

Phase 00–6 months
Test supply and access, not the plant
Binding term sheets for landed volume · vessel day cost established with the relevant authority · buyer letters of intent naming grade and volume · EU preference and US tariff eligibility confirmed in writing · labour and traceability baseline audit.
Phase 16–24 months
Toll first, own later
Process through existing capacity under toll agreement · establish grade recovery and yield on real landings · build buyer relationships on delivered product before committing to a plant.
Phase 224–48 months
Owned processing at proven scale
Plant sized to contracted supply, not to available fish · cold chain and power redundancy in initial scope · certification to buyer requirement.
Phase 348 months +
Move down the chain
Own-brand or private label · direct market access reducing tariff-jurisdiction dependence · by-product and loin-trade optionality.

Operating KPIs from first production: contracted supply ratio, plant utilisation, grade recovery, cold chain break incidents, landed cost per tonne including vessel day, certified output ratio, and days of finished inventory.

09

Market sizing: read with care

Published tuna market estimates are unusually inconsistent, and quoting any single one without the range would misrepresent what is actually known.

Published market size estimates, 2026 basis
ScopePublisher2026 estimateProjection
Tuna fish marketResearch and MarketsUS$37.26bnUS$43.66bn by 2030 · CAGR 4%

Canned tunaFortune Business InsightsUS$30.46bnUS$39.14bn by 2034 · CAGR 3.19%
Canned tunaGM InsightsUS$13.0bnUS$19.4bn by 2035 · CAGR ~4.8%
Canned tunaGrand View Research—US$29.90bn by 2033 · CAGR 4.2%
What this table actually shows

Two publishers put the 2026 canned tuna market at US$30.46bn and US$13.0bn — a difference of more than 2.3 times for the same segment in the same year. A third projects US$29.90bn for 2033, below the first publisher's 2026 figure.

These are not competing forecasts of the same quantity. They are different definitions of what counts as the market, published without reconciling scope. None of them should be used to size an investment. Size the opportunity from contracted volume, landed cost and realised price on your own numbers, and treat the tuna fish market figures the same way.

We reproduce the range rather than selecting the most attractive value. A report that quotes one of these without the others is not doing diligence on your behalf.

10

Recommendation

For the investment committee

Do not underwrite upstream capacity. The resource is healthy, effort is capped by agreement, and additional vessel days are a cost line. Underwrite downstream positions with contracted supply — and only where market access does not rest on a single tariff jurisdiction.

Conditions we would attach to capital release:

  • Binding landed-volume agreements before construction. Not letters of interest — term sheets with volume, grade and price mechanism.
  • Toll-processing proof in Phase 1. Establish grade recovery and buyer acceptance on real product before owning a plant.
  • Two market accesses minimum. A case resting solely on US tariff position or solely on EU preference is a policy bet.
  • Compliance treated as binary. Traceability and labour standards funded at Phase 0, with third-party audit before first shipment.
  • Sensitivity across vessel day cost, plant utilisation, grade recovery and tariff status. Point estimates on these should be treated as an incomplete submission.

The window is real. The tariff reordering has loosened processing geography for the first time in twenty years, and coastal states have both the resource and the fiscal motivation to move. But the same fiscal dependence that motivates them limits how much they can concede, and the redirected Thai and Vietnamese volume will meet any new entrant in the EU. This is an opening for operators who can secure fish and pass an audit — not for those competing on cost.
