The thesis in three sentences

The cashew trade has run for four decades on one arbitrage: Africa grows the raw nut, Asia shells it. That arbitrage is closing — not gradually, and not because of market forces alone, but because origin governments have made it policy and development banks have funded it.

The investable question is no longer whether processing moves to origin. It is which origin, at what quality position, and financed how — because in this industry working capital, not capital expenditure, decides who survives the first bad season.

01

Executive summary

Three things are true at once in the 2025–26 cashew market, and most published analysis captures only the first.

One — Vietnam still dominates, and got bigger. Vietnam exported roughly 766,600 tonnes of kernels for a record US$5.23bn in 2025, at an average realised price near US$6,817/tonne. It handles over 65% of world raw cashew nut (RCN) imports and supplies more than 80% of internationally traded kernels. Any thesis that treats Vietnam as displaced is wrong.

Two — Vietnam's margin structure is deteriorating. In the same year, Vietnam's RCN imports rose about 25% by volume but roughly 48% by value. Buying inputs at a faster rate of inflation than output prices is the textbook definition of a compressing spread. Vietnam also imported approximately 150,000 tonnes of semi-processed kernels in eleven months — equivalent to around 700,000 tonnes of RCN. Primary shelling is already migrating; the semi-processed import line is the evidence.

Three — origin processing is scaling, and it is policy-driven. Côte d'Ivoire processed roughly 600,000–660,000 tonnes domestically in 2025 against about 344,000 tonnes in 2024 — an increase between 67% and 92% depending on the reporting series. Benin, which banned raw cashew exports outright in April 2024, processed an estimated 50,000 tonnes of kernels in 2025, double the prior year. West African processing as a whole rose about 51% in 2025.

The investment implication is narrow and specific. The opportunity is not to build the lowest-cost bulk shelling line in West Africa — that race is being run by state-backed industrial zones with concessional finance. The opportunity is in the positions those zones do not fill well: quality-controlled, traceable, certification-ready primary processing, with by-product recovery designed in from day one rather than deferred.

02

Why the old map stopped working

The conventional description of this value chain — Africa produces, Asia processes, the West consumes — was accurate for a long time and is now a liability for anyone planning capital deployment around it.

The structural reason is physical. The shell accounts for roughly 70–75% of raw nut weight A. Exporting RCN means paying ocean freight, handling and financing costs on three-quarters of a cargo that will be discarded at destination. Every tonne of kernel shipped instead of raw nut removes about three tonnes of freight from the chain. That arithmetic did not change; what changed is that origin countries acquired the capital, policy instruments and political will to act on it.

Two planning conclusions follow. First, raw nut availability out of West Africa is now a political variable, not merely an agronomic one — a sourcing model built on assumed free export of RCN from any single origin carries policy risk that is not priced in most feasibility studies. Second, the direction is one-way but the pace is uneven: Benin has acted decisively, Côte d'Ivoire is using price signals rather than prohibition, and Ghana has announced more than it has implemented.

The capital behind the policy

Policy alone would not move volumes at this rate. Benin's Glo-Djigbé Industrial Zone (GDIZ) has drawn in excess of US$150m from the World Bank, the African Development Bank and ECOWAS, and is configured for 150,000–200,000 t/year of processing capacity with CNSL and biochar recovery designed into the zone rather than added later A. Côte d'Ivoire has developed three agro-industrial zones (ZAI) alongside shell-valorisation infrastructure under a World Bank competitiveness project A.

For a private investor this cuts both ways. Concessional capital lowers the sector's cost of entry and builds shared infrastructure — but it also means competing against balance sheets that are not required to earn a commercial return on the commodity line. That is the single strongest argument against entering as a bulk low-cost processor.

03

The Vietnam compression

Vietnam's position is frequently misread in both directions. It is neither collapsing nor secure.

The scale is genuine and growing: 3.12 million tonnes of RCN imported in 2025, more than 65% of world raw imports, and over 80% of globally traded kernels A. Domestic Vietnamese production supplies only around 10% of what its factories require — the model is, and always was, an import-process-export arbitrage rather than an agricultural one.

The vulnerability is in the spread. Import volume rose ~25% in 2025 while import value rose ~48% A. Realised export prices rose 13.9% to ~$6,817/t. Input costs inflated roughly three-and-a-half times faster than output prices. A processing business absorbs that only through yield, scale, or balance sheet — and it cannot absorb it indefinitely.

The tell most analyses miss

Vietnam imported approximately 150,000 tonnes of semi-processed kernels in eleven months of 2025 — the raw-nut equivalent of roughly 700,000 tonnes A.

A country with the world's deepest shelling capacity does not import semi-processed kernel unless the economics of shelling at home have deteriorated relative to shelling at origin. This is the clearest available signal that primary processing has already begun to relocate, with Vietnam retaining the higher-margin grading, sorting, packing and market-access layer.

That is the shape of the migration to underwrite: origin captures shelling; Asia retains finishing and distribution — for now.

Trade policy: what the number actually is

This point warrants correction because the wrong figure circulates widely. During the first half of 2025 a 46% tariff on Vietnamese imports was discussed as a negotiating scenario. It was not implemented. In October 2025 the USTR published a framework applying a 20% reciprocal tariff to Vietnamese-origin imports A.

Any model still running 46% materially overstates the case for relocating away from Vietnam. The risk is real but it is 20%, and a feasibility study built on the higher figure will not survive diligence.

04

Regulatory reality check

Compliance is where cashew feasibility studies most often go wrong, usually by importing assumptions from cocoa or coffee. The corrections below are not marginal — each changes the cost and timing of a compliance programme.

Common assumption

EUDR (EU Deforestation Regulation) governs cashew exports to the EU.

Position as at 2026

Cashew is not a covered commodity. EUDR Annex I lists cattle, cocoa, coffee, oil palm, rubber, soya and wood. Cashew appears on none of them A.

Common assumption

EUDR obligations began to bite in 2025.

Position as at 2026

Application was deferred once at end-2024 and again by later legislation; for large operators it is scheduled from 30 December 2026 A.

Common assumption

CSRD and CSDDD impose near-term direct reporting duties across the supply base.

Position as at 2026

CSRD wave 2 and 3 reporting deferred by two years; CSDDD first application deferred by one year (Directive (EU) 2025/794) A.

Common assumption

US tariff exposure on Vietnamese product is 46%.

Position as at 2026

The operative frame is a 20% reciprocal tariff per the USTR framework of October 2025 A.

None of this means traceability investment can be deferred. The binding constraint in this sector is not statute — it is private. Large retailers, branded manufacturers and their lenders already require geolocation of supply, documented labour due diligence, physical segregation and lot-level records as a condition of purchase. That functions as de facto regulation, it arrives earlier than the statutory timetable, and it is enforced by losing the account rather than by penalty.

The practical baseline is HACCP and ISO 22000, with BRCGS, SMETA, Fairtrade or Organic layered selectively by target channel rather than adopted wholesale.

05

Three origins, three different businesses

Treating West Africa as one sourcing geography is the most common structural error in this sector. The three principal origins are not variations on a theme — they support materially different business models, and the operating disciplines that make one work will not carry to another.

Côte d'Ivoire Scale hub

The largest RCN supply base in West Africa: ~1.2 Mt production (2023), three agro-industrial zones, and shell-valorisation infrastructure in development.

Plays to: volume procurement, plants sited adjacent to production, commodity-grade throughput.
Operating priority: securing early-season volume and processor-priority access before the export window opens.
Benin Integrated zero-waste hub

GDIZ is building toward 150,000–200,000 t/year with CNSL and biochar integrated by design. The raw export ban makes domestic processing structural rather than incentivised.

Plays to: industrial-zone tenancy, by-product monetisation, policy-aligned capital.
Operating priority: zone infrastructure leverage, warehousing and drying, simultaneous by-product lines.
Ghana Compliance-first premium hub

Regulated through TCDA with comparatively clear licensing. The World Bank rates Ghanaian RCN among West Africa's highest quality, but processing capacity has lagged and policy long favoured raw export.

Plays to: premium whole kernel, traceability, certification-led supply.
Operating priority: licensed local partner structure, lot-level traceability from day one, direct-export pilots.

Ghana: the case, and the constraint

Ghana is the origin most frequently misjudged, in both directions. It is not the cheapest raw material source in the region, and any model that enters on a low-cost premise will not hold. Minimum producer prices and export levies are set administratively — a stated minimum producer price of GH¢12/kg for the 2025/26 season, and a TCDA export levy of GHS 44.86/tonne A — which puts a floor under input cost by design.

The offsetting case is quality and clarity. The World Bank identifies Ghanaian RCN as among the top-quality raw nut in West Africa, with buyers demonstrably willing to pay a premium; the same analysis notes that processing capacity expansion stalled for years and that a policy environment favouring raw export compressed processor gross margins A.

There is also a hard structural constraint that belongs in any diligence checklist: foreign entities are restricted from direct farm-gate and district-level aggregation. A licensed Ghanaian partner structure is not a preference — it is a requirement, and it must be designed into the sourcing model and the traceability architecture from the outset rather than retrofitted A.

Read correctly, Ghana is a quality, traceability and certification business, not a volume business.

06

Unit economics: where margin is actually decided

Cashew processing is routinely underwritten as a capital expenditure business. It is not. It is a working capital and process-control business, and the failure modes cluster accordingly.

The metrics that determine the outcome

Where capital gets destroyed

Three errors recur often enough to be treated as a diligence checklist rather than as cautionary anecdotes.

  • Financing the plant and underestimating the season. Raw purchasing compresses into a 3–4 month window. A processor with adequate CAPEX and inadequate working capital buys poor lots late at inflated prices, or cannot buy at all and runs the line below capacity for a year.
  • Deferring warehousing, drying and segregated storage. These read as secondary spend and behave as primary risk. Inventory that degrades in store converts a good procurement year into a write-down.
  • Designing the kernel line only. Treating shell, energy recovery and CNSL as later-phase projects forfeits the downside hedge precisely when it is needed — in an oversupplied kernel market.
Underwriting implication

A feasibility model for this sector is incomplete without sensitivity analysis across KOR, breakage rate, inventory days, warehouse loss rate, FX and policy variation. Point estimates on any of these are not conservatism — they are an untested assumption in a business where each one independently determines the outcome.

07

By-products: the downside hedge

Kernel revenue alone does not defend long-run profitability, particularly through oversupplied phases of the price cycle. Since the shell represents 70–75% of raw weight, a processor that treats shell as waste is discarding the majority of its physical input and paying to dispose of it.

Sequencing matters more than ambition. Near term: broken kernels, shell as fuel, CNSL feasibility. Medium term: biochar and boiler energy integration. Longer term: milk, powder and apple-derived products — which are consumer businesses wearing an agro-processing costume, and should be underwritten as such.

A caution on CNSL market sizing

Published CNSL market estimates for 2025 diverge by more than an order of magnitude — from roughly US$0.51bn to US$3.46bn to US$6.96bn depending on the research house, with CAGRs quoted between 6.7% and 11.4% C.

A thirteen-fold spread between the low and high estimate means these figures are not measuring the same thing — definitions of the addressable market differ fundamentally between publishers. Do not underwrite a CNSL investment on any of them. Size the opportunity bottom-up from the shell tonnage your own plant will generate and from firm offtake discussions, not from a top-down market number.

We flag this rather than quoting the most attractive figure. Any report that cites one of these numbers without the range is not doing diligence on your behalf.

Phased entry: proving before building

Raw material quality, licensed aggregation structures, storage performance, buyer willingness to pay and by-product economics are all systematically overestimated in the absence of field data. Each is cheap to test and expensive to assume. The sequencing below places verification ahead of capital commitment deliberately.

Phase 00–6 months
Test the investment hypothesis
Legal and regulatory due diligence in-country · tolling trials with existing capacity · measured KOR and breakage on real lots · letters of intent from named buyers · warehouse and power availability survey.
Phase 16–18 months
Premium primary processing in operation
Shelling, grading and packing at a single site · HACCP / ISO 22000 certification · digital lot-level traceability · backup power installed, not planned.
Phase 218–36 months
Diversify the revenue base
CNSL or shell-energy pilot · direct export of premium whole kernel · B2B ingredient line extension.
Phase 336 months +
Multi-site, multi-product
Scale-up linked to Côte d'Ivoire or Benin subject to demonstrated demand · retail and private label · deeper zero-waste integration.

Operating KPIs from first production

Seven measures are sufficient to run this business and necessary to run it well: RCN outturn (KOR), whole kernel ratio, breakage rate, warehouse loss, inventory days, certified output ratio, and by-product recovery ratio. Reporting that omits inventory days and warehouse loss will show a profitable operation shortly before it shows a write-down.

10

Demand: the case for the output

Supply-side theses fail when demand is assumed rather than examined. The demand picture here is supportive but should be stated with its uncertainty intact.

Published global cashew market sizing clusters between roughly US$8.5bn and US$10.6bn for 2026, with 2030–2034 projections spanning US$11.9bn to US$14.6bn and CAGRs quoted between 4.6% and 6.7% B. The spread reflects differing definitions of scope rather than genuine disagreement about direction. Global kernel consumption is forecast to approach 1.56 million tonnes by 2030 B.

Growth is concentrated in Asia rather than in the traditional Western markets. Chinese tree-nut consumption is rising around 5% annually, with per-capita use projected to reach roughly 1 kg by 2030 — close to double 2023 levels B. India is simultaneously the largest consumer and a major processor, which makes it a demand centre and a competitor at once.

For an origin-processing investment two demand characteristics matter more than headline growth. First, certified organic kernel commands a 15–20% premium in US and European retail C — which is the economic argument for the certification spend in Phase 1. Second, the fastest-growing applications are ingredient-led rather than snack-led: butter, milk, powder and plant-based formats. Ingredient buyers purchase on specification and traceability, which favours precisely the compliance-first position this playbook recommends.

11

Recommendation

For the investment committee

Approve origin processing only as a premium, traceable, compliance-led platform — never as a bulk commodity line. The bulk position is occupied by state-backed zones operating on concessional capital, and it cannot be won on cost.

Conditions we would attach to any capital release:

  • Phase 0 completed before CAPEX. Tolling trials, measured outturn on real lots, and buyer LOIs in hand — not modelled.
  • Sourcing through a licensed local partner structure, with cross-border mixed-origin material tracked as a distinct traceability risk rather than absorbed into general supply.
  • Plant designed as a complete operation — storage, drying, backup power and waste-to-energy recovery in the initial scope, not deferred to a later phase.
  • HACCP / ISO 22000 as the floor, with BRCGS, SMETA, Fairtrade or Organic added by target channel rather than adopted wholesale.
  • Sensitivity analysis mandatory across KOR, breakage, inventory days, warehouse loss, FX and policy variation. Point estimates on these variables should be treated as an incomplete submission.

The window here is real but it is not indefinite. Origin processing capacity is being built now with concessional capital and policy protection. The positions that remain available to commercial investors in three years will be narrower and more expensive than those available today — but only for entrants who can demonstrate quality control and traceability, because that is the one thing the state-backed zones are not optimised to deliver.
